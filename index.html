About

AI systems are starting to improve themselves. Language-model agents rewrite their own prompts, memory, tools, and code; evolutionary coding agents keep the program variants that score better on an automatic evaluator; and models trained with reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards improve on problems whose answers can be checked. This loop is known as recursive self-improvement (RSI).

Science and engineering are a natural testbed for RSI. Many scientific and engineering problems come with checks that do not depend on human judgment: a simulator, a conservation law, a numerical solver, a proof checker, a wet-lab assay, or a held-out measurement. These checks supply the reward signal an improving system needs, and they make it possible to tell real progress from reward hacking.

The symposium brings together two communities that rarely meet: AI researchers who build self-improving systems, and the scientists, mathematicians, and engineers whose problems come with reliable checks. Our two guiding questions are:

  1. How can self-improving AI systems accelerate discovery and design in science and engineering, and how should we measure that they do?
  2. What do scientific and engineering problems teach us about building safer and more effective self-improving systems?

Topics

Topics include, but are not limited to:

Self-improving agents for discovery and design

Agents that revise their own code, tools, prompts, memory, or skill libraries while working on scientific or engineering tasks.

Automated algorithm and method discovery

LLM-guided evolutionary and program search for numerical methods, solvers, optimization heuristics, and scientific software.

Learning from verifiable scientific rewards

Simulators, physical constraints, formal proofs, and experimental feedback as training signals.

Self-generated curricula and data

Self-play, problem generation, and synthetic data for science and mathematics.

Evaluation of RSI

Benchmarks that stay informative as systems improve; contamination; reward hacking and verifier exploits.

Closed-loop and embodied discovery

Self-improving models coupled with laboratory automation and high-throughput experiments.

Statistical and decision-theoretic foundations

Uncertainty quantification and valid inference for results selected by an improving search.

Safety, oversight, and governance

Monitoring, sandboxing, and human oversight of self-modifying systems in high-stakes science.

Case studies

Physics, chemistry, materials, biology and medicine, climate science, operations research, and engineering.

Format

The symposium follows the AAAI in-person policy: all keynote speakers, presenters, and panelists present in person. We aim for a program in which discussion takes as much time as presentation.

Keynotes

Three one-hour keynotes, one per day, balanced between AI researchers building self-improving systems and domain scientists using AI on problems with strong verifiers.

Contributed talks and posters

Short papers (up to 8 pages including references) and extended abstracts (2 pages) in AAAI style, each reviewed by at least two program committee members. We expect about eight contributed talks and about 35 posters.

Target problems

We will post four to six open problems from science and engineering, each with a public statement, data, and an automatic checker. Participants may bring self-improving systems to these problems before or during the symposium; results, including failures and verifier exploits, are presented on Day 2.

Breakout working groups

Mixed groups led by an AI researcher and a domain scientist discuss (a) evaluating self-improving systems, (b) which tasks have reliable verifiers and how to build them, and (c) oversight and reproducibility of results from self-improving agents.

Panels

Three open-format panels with audience questions, one per day.
